HOW A STORY IS TOLD HERE — these rules are what separate a film from a list of facts:
1. ONE THROUGHLINE. Every beat moves the protagonist toward or away from what they want. If a sentence does not change the situation, cut it.
2. SAY EVERYTHING ONCE. A fact, a date, a number, a character's introduction, a place's description, an image — each is stated exactly one time in the whole film. Later chapters never recap, never re-establish, never "return to" earlier material; they may refer back in three or four words at most. Re-read your draft for repeats before you finish.
3. EVENTS, NOT INVENTORY. Show the world through what people DO in it — decisions, failures, discoveries, confrontations, small victories — never through lists of objects ("Green felt. Brass ashtrays. A wall clock."). No descriptive sentences at all: a place is named, never described (rule 16).
4. CAUSE AND EFFECT. Consecutive beats connect by "because" and "so", never by "and then". The end of each beat creates the need for the next.
5. CONCRETE AND VISUALIZABLE — real actions, places, people, moments. No abstract commentary ("that is the correction", "this is how power starts", "in many ways"), no lessons stated mid-story: the meaning is carried by the events. If the genre wants a lesson said aloud, it is said once, at the very end.
6. SENTENCES of 8-20 words — complete, flowing, spoken. Write in BEATS of ~20-22 words (one sentence, or a pair of short ones), because the text is cut into 8-second scenes; an idea that needs several beats keeps them consecutive so it never breaks mid-scene.
7. PROGRESSION. Time and place move forward and the viewer always knows when and where they are; no chapter re-opens in the moment a previous chapter opened.
8. Flowing narration only — no headings other than the chapter markers, no scene numbers, no meta text.
9. NO GLOSSARY. Never define a term in its own sentence ("A pile is a long structural element…", "Reclamation means placing fill…", "A cantilever is a part that…"). The viewer understands a thing by watching it DO something with a number attached ("crews drive 230 concrete piles forty metres down, into sand that no longer moves"). An editor COUNTS definitions after you finish; two is a failure.
10. NO META. The narration never mentions the film, the camera, the viewer, a chapter, a turning point, "this video", "our story", or what the film is about to do ("only now does the film move inside", "the camera enters the atrium", "this is turning point four"). The audience is watching a story, not a description of one; the spine's turning points are for YOU, never for them.
11. NO SIGNPOSTING. A beat never opens with a connector whose only job is to steer ("Now the work shifts…", "So the question is…", "At this point…", "By the end of this stage…", "Then came…"). Cut hard from one particular to the next; the ORDER carries the logic. Over one beat in ten opening that way is counted as a failure.
12. EVERY BEAT CARRIES A PARTICULAR the viewer could not have guessed — a number, a name, a date, a measurement, a price, a distance, a consequence. A sentence that could sit in any film about anything ("the ambition was extreme", "this was a huge challenge", "that changes everything") is deleted and replaced by the specific thing that made it so. Difficulty is a concrete risk with a concrete consequence ("if the core passes 70°C the slab cracks before the tower exists"), never an adjective.
13. ONE TURN PER CHAPTER. Each chapter contains at least one reversal — a fact or an event that contradicts what the previous beats led the viewer to expect — placed, never announced. A chapter that only continues is a chapter the viewer leaves.
14. THE VOICE IS SHARP, NOT FRIENDLY. No chummy asides, no reassurance, no "you can feel how", no tidy summaries that repeat what was just shown. Cool, exact and quietly amazed — the register of someone who knows how the thing works and still cannot quite believe it does.
15. THE LAST CHAPTER LANDS. The film ends on a CLOSING BEAT — the final two or three sentences of the LAST chapter — and it is the one place in the script where meaning may be stated. It answers the question the film opened with, using this film's own particulars: the last event, and the consequence that outlives it — what is true now that was not true at the start. It is NOT a summary of the chapters, NOT a moral addressed to the viewer, NOT a new fact, and NOT a camera position: a film that stops on where the camera rests ("…while the light fills the water", "…while the crowd closes around him") stops without ending, and an editor CHECKS the last sentence for exactly that shape. Write the close as a statement, not as a shot.
16. THE VOICE SAYS WHAT THE PICTURE CANNOT. The viewer is already looking at the scene, so the narration NEVER describes what is in frame — no appearances, surfaces, colours, light, weather, textures, camera positions, and no play-by-play of visible motion ("the road climbs above the river, slick under flashing lamps", "sparks spit through spray"). It carries what a picture cannot show: what somebody wants, decides, risks, loses or learns; the cause; the cost; the number; what is about to go wrong; what it will mean later. A place or a time may be NAMED ("the harbour, near dusk"), never described. Real scripts carry ZERO texture words per hundred; an editor COUNTS them after you finish. (The one exception is a SILENT film, whose text is never spoken: there Voice Mode's beat-sheet rules replace this one.)
