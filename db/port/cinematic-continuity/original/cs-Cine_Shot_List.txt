THE CRAFT — what makes a sequence cinematic instead of a row of pictures:
1. COVERAGE. Build every sequence the way a camera crew covers a scene: a frame that places us (wide, aerial, a strong silhouette against the sky), then into the action (medium, tracking with it), then the detail that makes it tactile (an insert or macro: hands on a tool, the blade biting into clay, water beading on paint, a gauge needle rising). Change the SIZE of the shot at every cut — wide, medium, close, extreme close, and back out. Two neighbouring shots of the same size from the same side read as a jump cut.
2. ONE SHOT, ONE IDEA. Eight seconds hold one image and one movement: one subject doing one thing, and one camera move. The video model renders one moving thing convincingly, so the energy goes into the camera, the light and the sound — a single car passing a still landscape, one pair of hands at work, a flag snapping while everything behind it is still. A line that needs "then" is two shots: keep the one that matters, or give each its own line.
3. A MOTIVATED CAMERA. Every move has a reason, and you name it: track WITH what travels, push IN toward what matters, pull back or crane UP to reveal scale, orbit a still subject to show its shape, or stay locked-off when the subject's own motion is the drama. Slow moves for calm sequences, faster and closer ones for peak sequences.
4. LIGHT. Every shot names its light — source, hour, quality and direction ("low gold sun raking from the left", "blue hour sky with warm lamplight in the windows", "hard noon straight overhead, black shadows"). Follow the treatment's visual arc: the hour and the light move across the film, and the last sequence looks unmistakably different from the first.
5. SCREEN DIRECTION. Whatever travels keeps travelling the same way across the frame within a sequence (left to right stays left to right, toward the camera stays toward the camera) unless a shot shows it turning. Name the direction every time something travels.
6. TEXTURE AND SCALE. Alternate the intimate and the vast: the grain of the material, then the whole landscape it belongs to. The viewer should feel how things are made, how big they are and how far they go.
7. SOUND. Every shot names its dominant sound — the chip of a spade, rain on canvas, an engine note climbing, wind across a ridge, one sound in a silence. It is the only audio the film has besides music.
8. PICTURES ONLY. Everything in a line can be seen or heard. A reason becomes an action a body performs, a cost becomes something visibly lost, a decision becomes a hand that stops. Years, numbers, names of things and explanations belong to narrated films; here a figure is known by what they do, their faces may stay turned to the work, their mouths stay closed, and every sign, page and screen in frame is blank or turned away.
9. THE CUT BETWEEN SEQUENCES. The first shot of each sequence is its OPENS ON frame and answers the last shot of the one before — a match cut (round tunnel mouth → round window; a spinning wheel → a spinning sun), a sound bridge, or a hard contrast of light or scale. The last shot of each sequence is its ENDS WITH frame.
10. THE SIGNATURE IMAGES. Each of the treatment's signature images appears in its sequence, composed as the most beautiful frame of the film: symmetry, silhouette, leading lines, a foreground frame, real depth.
11. THE LAST SHOT is the treatment's ending image: a composed, held frame with one slow movement, so the film lands instead of stopping.
12. PROCESS AND ORDER. When the film shows something being made, travelled or transformed, the shots follow its real order, and a place appears only in the state the work has reached (the Story Bible's states, in order).
